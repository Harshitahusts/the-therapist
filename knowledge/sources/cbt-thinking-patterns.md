# Thinking patterns in cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)

## The basic idea
CBT is built on the observation that situations, thoughts, emotions, body sensations and behaviours influence each other. The same event can feel very different depending on how we interpret it. Noticing our interpretations, and checking them against the evidence, can loosen the grip of painful feelings.

## Common unhelpful thinking patterns
- All-or-nothing thinking: seeing things in extremes ("If this isn't perfect, it's a failure").
- Catastrophising: jumping to the worst outcome ("I'll get fired and never work again").
- Mind reading: assuming we know what others think ("They think I'm incompetent").
- Overgeneralising: one event becomes a rule ("This always happens to me").
- Discounting the positive: dismissing what went well ("That only worked out by luck").
- Should statements: rigid rules that create guilt or pressure ("I should be further along by now").
- Emotional reasoning: treating a feeling as proof ("I feel like a fraud, so I must be one").
- Labelling: summarising yourself with a harsh label ("I'm a loser") instead of describing a specific event.

Everyone thinks this way sometimes, especially when tired, stressed or low. Noticing a pattern is not a criticism; it is a first step towards a more balanced view.

## A simple thought check
1. What happened? Describe the situation briefly and factually.
2. What went through your mind? Identify the main thought.
3. How did you feel, and how strongly (0 to 100)?
4. What supports the thought? What doesn't fit with it?
5. What would you say to a friend who had this thought?
6. Is there a more balanced way to see it? Re-rate the feeling.

The aim is not "positive thinking" but accurate, fair thinking.

## Useful questions
- "Is there another way to look at this?"
- "What's the most likely outcome, not just the worst?"
- "If it did happen, how would I cope?"
- "Will this matter in a year?"
