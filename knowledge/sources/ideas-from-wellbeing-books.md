# Key ideas from well-known wellbeing books

These are short summaries, in our own words, of ideas from widely read books. They are pointers to explore, not substitutes for the books or for professional care.

## Viktor Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning (1946)
Frankl, a psychiatrist who survived Nazi concentration camps, argued that the search for meaning is a central human drive. He described three sources of meaning: creating or doing something (work, a deed), experiencing something or loving someone, and the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering. Even when circumstances can't be changed, we keep some freedom in how we respond. A useful question from his approach: "What is life asking of me right now?"

## David D. Burns, Feeling Good (1980)
Burns brought cognitive behavioural therapy to a wide audience. Its core idea is that moods are strongly shaped by the thoughts we have about events. He named common "cognitive distortions", such as all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, labelling and "should" statements, and suggested writing down an upsetting thought, spotting the distortion, and writing a more balanced, realistic response.

## Jon Kabat-Zinn, Full Catastrophe Living (1990)
Kabat-Zinn created Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR). Mindfulness here means paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, without judging what we notice. Practices include the body scan, mindful breathing and gentle movement. The aim is not to empty the mind but to relate differently to stress and pain, responding rather than reacting.

## Kristin Neff, Self-Compassion (2011)
Neff describes self-compassion as having three parts: kindness toward ourselves instead of harsh judgement, recognising that struggle is part of the shared human experience instead of feeling isolated, and mindful awareness of pain without exaggerating or suppressing it. Her research suggests self-compassion supports resilience and motivation rather than making people complacent.

## Martin Seligman, Flourish (2011)
Seligman, a founder of positive psychology, describes wellbeing with the PERMA model: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment. One well-known exercise is "three good things": each evening, write down three things that went well and why they happened. Over time this can shift attention toward what is going right.

## Brené Brown, The Gifts of Imperfection (2010) and Daring Greatly (2012)
Brown's research looks at shame, vulnerability and worthiness. She argues that vulnerability (uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure) is not weakness but the root of connection, creativity and courage. Shame grows in secrecy and silence; speaking about it with someone who responds with empathy helps it lose its grip.

## Steven C. Hayes, Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life (2005)
Hayes developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). Instead of fighting difficult thoughts and feelings, ACT encourages making room for them, stepping back from thoughts ("I'm having the thought that..."), clarifying personal values, and taking small committed actions toward those values.

## Thich Nhat Hanh, The Miracle of Mindfulness (1975)
The Vietnamese Zen teacher wrote about bringing full attention to ordinary moments, such as washing the dishes just to wash the dishes. Conscious breathing, noticing each in-breath and out-breath, is offered as a simple anchor to return to the present when emotions are strong.

## Rick Hanson, Hardwiring Happiness (2013)
Hanson describes the brain's "negativity bias": negative experiences tend to stick, while positive ones slip away. His suggestion, "taking in the good", is to notice a good moment, stay with it for a few breaths, and let it sink in, so that small positive experiences add up over time.

## Susan David, Emotional Agility (2016)
David encourages naming emotions precisely ("disappointed", "lonely", "overwhelmed" rather than just "bad") and noticing them with curiosity: "I'm noticing that I'm feeling...". Precise naming creates a little space between feeling and action, making it easier to choose a response that fits our values.
