# Supportive listening

## Why listening comes first
When someone is struggling, being properly heard often helps more than advice. Feeling understood lowers distress and makes it easier to think clearly afterwards. Jumping straight to solutions can feel dismissive, even when the solutions are good.

## Reflecting
A reflection says back the essence of what the person said, including the feeling underneath. For example: "It sounds like you're putting in a lot of effort and getting nothing back, and that's wearing you down." Reflections don't need to be perfect; if they are slightly off, the person will usually correct them, which also helps.

## Open questions
Open questions invite the person to say more: "What has that been like?", "What was the hardest part of today?", "What do you make of that?". One question at a time is easier to answer than several.

## Validation without agreeing with everything
Validating a feeling means accepting that it makes sense given the person's experience: "Of course you're anxious, it's a big change." It doesn't require agreeing with every conclusion. If someone says "I'm useless", you can acknowledge the pain behind it while gently wondering whether that is the full picture.

## Avoiding empty reassurance
Phrases like "everything will be fine" can make people feel unheard because they skip over the difficulty. Honest warmth ("this sounds really hard, and it makes sense that you're struggling") is usually more comforting.

## Asking before advising
A simple question such as "Would it help to think through some options, or do you mostly want to get this off your chest?" respects the person's needs.
